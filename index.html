Inquiry focus

How has the changing nature of work shaped Honeysuckle, and where might it be heading next?

Students identify the jobs and work arrangements connected to different industries before exploring how employment affects wellbeing and community life. They then evaluate how stakeholders influence redevelopment decisions and use a fieldwork visit to connect the local site to broader questions about change over time.

Teacher portal

Teaching sequence

These lessons use Honeysuckle as a case study to help students identify how the work landscape has changed, examine why these changes affect individuals and communities, and then analyse how different stakeholders influence the changes made. The excursion brings these ideas together and gives students evidence to connect historical change to present-day land use and future planning.

Lesson planning

Questions & Teaching Decisions

Why Honeysuckle?

It offers a visible example of industrial change and redevelopment in one location. Students can connect visible landscape shifts with broader issues around jobs, economic change and local planning.

How the lessons fit together

Lesson 1 introduces work and work arrangements; Lesson 2 explores how industrial change affects wellbeing and identity; Lesson 3 adds stakeholder negotiation and trade-offs.

Checking understanding

Student learning is checked through classification decisions, discussions, exit tickets, quizzes, fieldwork observations and a final proposal.

Teacher lesson guides

Lesson pages

Lesson 1 — Types of work

Inquiry: How have the types of work and work arrangements in Honeysuckle changed over time?

  • Industry Workforce Challenge
  • Honeysuckle Worker Sort
  • Industry Collision

Teaching intention: Students build a foundation in identifying jobs, work arrangements and the economic shifts associated with industrial change.

Why this approach?

Students can start with familiar occupations before they examine a less familiar historical setting. The fictional worker cards let students interpret employment clues instead of simply memorising definitions, and the industry collision allows them to predict how changing land use could affect work over time.

Lesson 2 — Work and wellbeing

Inquiry: How has industrial change in Newcastle affected the wellbeing of individuals and the broader community?

  • Workshop closure discussion
  • Research source analysis
  • Opinion line

Teaching intention: Students connect economic change to identity, social life and community wellbeing.

Why this approach?

The BHP closure in Mayfield provides a significant Newcastle example of industrial change and its effects on workers and communities. Historical sources help students consider evidence and different experiences instead of assuming redevelopment has only positive or only negative effects, while the opinion line gives them a way to justify a position and listen to different perspectives.

Lesson 3 — Stakeholders

Inquiry: How do different stakeholders influence decisions about redevelopment, and whose interests are prioritised?

  • Stakeholder roles
  • Development meeting
  • Decision matrix

Teaching intention: Students negotiate trade-offs and explain how competing interests shape decision-making around place.

Why this approach?

Stakeholder roles make students consider priorities other than their own. Distinguishing needs and wants gives them a structure for deciding what can and cannot be compromised, while unexpected developments reveal that plans often require revision when costs, heritage, investment or environmental concerns become more visible.
